White Noise

White noise is what most people first think of when they here random noise. By far, white noise is the simplest algorithm used to generate unpredictable sensory feedback. It naturally appears anywhere on surfaces or noises that contains uncontrolled randomness at every sample interval.

White noise orginally got it's name as an analogy of 'white light'. Similar to how white light is the mixture of all visible colors, white noise is described as a mixture of all audible frequencies at equal intensity. This is unlike Color Noises, which are a mixture of frequencies at a biased sample of intensities.

However, unlike how it may be described, white noise is not created by generating all audible frequencies and piling them ontop of each other. Instead, a shortcut is used: we randomly generate a value for each sample interval of the noise pattern. Every value must be equally possible to randomly select, for each interval, in order it to count as White Noise.

Sounds

White noise sounds, like what you may be familar with in your traditional YouTube Reels, are an example of a 1D noise.

In the most basic sense, this type of noise is an ordered list of randomized values, each within the audible spectrum of sound and equally possible to randomly select. Due to this randomness, however, the appearence of white noise is often a collection jagged and unpredictable connected lines, and why you hear "static" when these ordered list of values is played as sound.

Textures

White noise textures, meanwhile, are 2D noises. These cannot be repsented as sounds or a singled ordered list, like a white noise sounds could. Instead, these are closer to the snow you'd get from a TV screen displaying the surrounding electromagnetic waves without a filter.

These are generated essentially the exact same way as sounds were: randomly generating values within a range of vaild numbers, each with an equal random probability of being generated. This time, however, we generate these values as a 2D grid of values, instead of a 1D list.

These values are then processed as individual pixels within a 2D texture image. Ignoring color, each number represents the light intensity of the pixel in grayscale. The higher the number, the whiter the pixel. The lower, the darker. As can be seen, there is no smoothness: each pixel's brightness is independent to it's neighbors. This is the expected outcome, as each pixel's value is independently generated from every others.
